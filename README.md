# Visual Illusion Task for Adults

A new version of the visual illusion task which includes 8 different illusions.

Our set includes Ebbinghaus (Ebbinghaus, 1902), Delboeuf (Delboeuf, 1865), Müller-Lyer (Müller-Lyer, 1889) and Ponzo (Ponzo, 1910) as geometrical illusions to represent two area and two longitudinal geometrical form, the Simultaneous Contrast illusion (Contrast from here onwards; Chevreul, 1839), a black and white version of the Moving Snakes illusion (Kitaoka, 2003), the Kanizsa Illusory Contour illusion (Kanizsa from here onwards; Kanizsa, 1955) and the Solitaire illusion (Frith & Frit, 1972) as a numerosity illusion. 

## Probe trials

These trials were built in at the beginning of the program to familiarize participants with the test and to check that they understood the instructions. First, they had to select the longer line out of seven, random direction and position. It was repeated three times. Second, they had to click on a green circle. which appeared in a random position on the screen. The circle gradually shrank after every click (10 repeats).

The third type of probe trial was built just before the Kanizsa illusion trials. Four pictures appeared simultaneously on the screen: a Kanizsa square in which all Pac-man-shaped inducers were in the right position to form a square, a Kanizsa square in which one of the Pac-man-shaped inducers were turned out forward, a Kanizsa square in which two of the Pac-man-shaped inducers were turned out forward, and a Kanizsa triangle. The participant had to choose the picture with the square. The program did not let the user continue until the choice was correct. This was repeated two times.

Geometrical [Ebbinghaus, Delboeuf, Müller-Lyer, and Ponzo] illusions: In these illusions the geometric properties of a stimulus – e.g., size, angle, area, or form – are affected and systematically altered by the presence of other elements in the visual field. Every comparison in all geometric illusions is presented one time, which makes a total of 18 trials for each of the geometrical illusions. 

## Ebbinghaus illusion

It consists of an inner circle surrounded by an annulus of either larger or smaller circles. The central circle is perceived as smaller when surrounded by larger circles than when surrounded by smaller circles.

*Control conditions*: Different-sized target circles are surrounded by identically sized (small or large) outer circles on both sides (Small and Large controls). The two target circles always differ in size—by 1%, 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, or 30%—and this difference varies across trials.

*Illusory conditions*: the larger inner circle is surrounded by larger outer circles, and the smaller inner circle is surrounded by smaller outer circles. Illusion perception leads to a decrease in performance due to the overestimation of the smaller target circle and the underestimation of the larger circle. 

The task for the participants is to decide whether the larger center circle was on the left or the right.

## Delboeuf illusion

It consists of an inner circle surrounded by an either larger or small annulus ring. The central circle is perceived as smaller when surrounded by a larger ring than when surrounded by smaller one. 

*Control conditions*: Different-sized target circles are surrounded by identically sized (small or large) outer ring on both sides (Small and Large controls). 
The two target circles always differ in size—by 1%, 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, or 30%—and this difference varies across trials.

*Illusory conditions*: The larger inner circle is surrounded by a larger ring, and the smaller inner circle is surrounded by a smaller ring. Illusion perception leads to a decrease in performance due to the overestimation of the smaller target circle and the underestimation of the larger circle. 

The task for the participants is to decide whether the larger center circle was on the left or the right.

## Müller-Lyer illusion

It consists of horizontal lines that have inward- or outward-pointing fins on both ends. The length of a line is perceptually overestimated if bounded by outward-pointing fins and perceptually underestimated if bounded by inward-pointing fins.

*Control conditions*: Different-length target lines are bound by fins pointing inward or outward on both sides (Inward and Outward controls). The two target lines always differ in size—by 1%, 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, or 30%—and this difference varies across trials.

*Illusory conditions*: The fins of the different length lines are arranged so that the context would oppose accurate discrimination; the longer target line has fins pointing inward, making it appear shorter, whereas the shorter target line has fins pointing outward making it appear longer. 

The task for the participants is to decide whether the longer line was on the left or on the right. 
 
## Ponzo illusion

A geometric visual illusion commonly presented as two horizontal parallel lines, equal in length, above one another, surrounded by a set of converging lines. These converging lines represent an intersecting feature in the display that distort the perception of the equally sized parallel lines such that the upper line (closest to the apex of the converging lines) appears to be longer in length than the lower line. The two target lines always differ in size—by 1%, 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, or 30%—and this difference varies across trials. 
*Control conditions*: Target lines of different lengths are presented in the context of non-converging surrounding lines. 
*Illusory conditions*: Target lines of different lengths are presented with converging vertical surrounding lines; the shorter target line placed in the lower position, making it appear longer than the longer line which is in the upper position. 

The task for the participants is to decide whether the longer line was on above or below. 


## Simultaneous Contrast illusion
This illusion exploits the mechanisms underlying lightness constancy. The perception of the luminance of a stimulus – how dark or light – is affected by the immediate surroundings. Two target objects which are the same shade of grey are perceived different depending on the shade of their surroundings.

*Control conditions*: Target circles of different shades of gray are exhibited in front of rectangles that have the same luminance (both darker or both lighter than the target circles). The difference in the target circles’ luminance is 1, 2, 4, 6 and 10 units on a gray scale where 0 is black and 100 is white. Half of the control trials are presented on darker; the other half are presented on lighter backgrounds. 

*Illusory conditions*:  Different-shade target circles are exhibited in front of different-shade (darker or lighter) rectangles. The rectangles are arranged so that the context opposes accurate discrimination: the darker inner circle has the darker rectangle, while the lighter target circle has the light rectangle as background.

The task for the participants is to decide whether the darker center circle was on the left or the right. In total 15 Contrast illusion trials are presented to the participants: ten different control (five light and five dark control) and 5 different illusory trials. 

## Numerosity Solitaire illusion

This illusion occurs when elements of two different colors appear more numerous when clustered together centrally, but less numerous when un-clustered.
We have prepared a matrix of 115 dots in a ‘+’ shape arrangement, where we can modify the number and arrangement of white and black dots. Participants will be presented with pairs of matrices containing different quantities and arrangement of black dots and asked which they think contains more. In the 
*Control condition*: different numbers of dots are arranged randomly on both sides. 
*Illusory condition*: the dots on the side with more dots are arranged toward the periphery (un-clustered). 

The task for the participants is to decide whether the more numerous black dots was on the left or the right. In total 15 Contrast illusion trials are presented to the participants: 10 different control (5 light and 5 dark control) and 5 different illusory trials. 

## Moving Snake illusion

A type of peripheral drift illusion, the "snakes" consist of periodical arrangement of regions with different alternating luminance in several bands which resemble coiled serpents. Although the image is static, certain luminance sequence produces illusory motion perception and the snakes appear to be moving in circles. 
Five different versions of the Moving snake illusion are created where the patches of the snake have different illuminances. Contrast was gradually reduced between the two shades of gray in five equal steps. More contrast between the patches separated by the white or black line results in a stronger illusion magnitude. 

The task for the participants is to decide whether they perceive movement or not. In total 10 pictures were presented to the participants.

## Kanizsa Illusory Contour illusions (or subjective contours)

These illusions evoke the perception of an edge without a luminance or color change across that edge. We chose the Kanizsa square for our tests.
We prepared pictures of a grid of Pac-man shaped inducers (7 x 7) where the orientation of the mouth of the Pac-men was randomized. Two different versions were created, one where four Pac-man shapes next to each other created a Kanizsa square somewhere in the grid and one where no contour illusion appeared. Then these two pictures were placed next to each other. Participants were asked to choose the pair of images presented at the same time in which they saw a square. Since the size of the Pac-Man-shaped inducers can influence the magnitude of the illusion perception (larger the Pac-man, stronger the illusion), we created 5 types of pictures where the size of the Pac-man shapes was altered. Since the Kanizsa square appeared randomly, which might influence the performance (e.g. easier to spot the square if it is in the middle of the screen) we tested five times with each size thus we ran 25 trials in total.
